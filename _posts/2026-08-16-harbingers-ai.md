_Opportunities exist on the frontiers where most men dare not venture, and it is often the case that the farther the frontier, the greater the opportunity._

Artificial Intelligence is the defining technological paradigm of our lifetimes. After close to 75 years of being on the fringes, AI is now firmly part of the zeitgeist. It's also the right time for a new AI lab.  

But first, a short note on how we got here.  

AI has a rich history. People have wanted to create sentient machines since the 1940s (McCulloch–Pitts neuron). Alan Turing came up with the classic Turing Test and I.J. Good thought of the consequences of the intelligence explosion (colloquially RSI). The Dartmouth conference had a timeboxed plan to build sentient AI but drastically underestimated the timelines. Joseph Weizenbaum created ELIZA which was similar to a human chat operator and indeed fooled many humans. Weizenbaum then spent his career worrying about the societal aspects of AI. Minsky and Papert wrote Perceptrons in the late 60s and effectively killed neural networks for atleast a decade. Instead, expert systems proliferated which were mainly handcrafted rule engines and fact stores, like Dendral and Cyc. The early programming languages associated with AI were also different from modern ones. Lisp and Prolog were the instruments of choice - being functional and logic oriented, the programming model was different from the imperative/object-oriented ones we're used to, now. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams made a breakthrough by training neural networks via an backpropagation in the mid 80s. Stochastic optimization was used even back then, with roots as far back as the 1950s with the Widrow-Hoff delta rule for training ADALINE. Even outside the US, neural networks were being trained via backprop, like Fukushima's Neocognitron and Schmidhuber's lab in Europe.  

So the algorithms have been in place for a long time but what was lacking was the hardware horsepower and the data. GPUs were non-existent then, even though there were computers specifically for training neural networks, like CNAPS. The data problem was solved by the advent of the world wide web and big data. The hardware problem was solved by Nvidia who caught on early to the potential of AI by staying in close touch with scientists using GPUs for intensive computation, by translating neural network primitives to graphics primitives.  

But it still took two decades. The late 90s/early 2000s saw the explosion of kernel-based methods. Support vector machines, decision trees, logistic regression, graphical models, Bayesian causal inference etc. were getting better results than neural networks. The term artificial intelligence became so perjorative after multiple "AI winters" that people working on AI started referring to their work as data mining or machine learning. The switch to neural networks happened via the ImageNet contest in 2012. The groundwork had actually been laid a few years before, when Fei-Fei Li realized that computer vision lacked a rigorous evaluation benchmark and built the largest dataset till date via crowdsourcing. Alex Krizhevsky trained a neural network on a GPU and beat the previous top score by a huge margin. In 2010, something similar happened with speech recognition when Hinton and collaborators beat Gaussian mixture models with deep neural networks.  

The next decade saw an almost-overnight switch to deep learning. Neural networks were crushing alternatives at most tasks. Even within neural networks, there was a wide variety: feedforward, CNNs, GANs, RNNs (LSTMs)... Bahdanau and later Luong came up with the attention mechanism for sequence learning. The famous 2017 Transformers paper took most of the ideas that had been circulating then, threw most of the extraneous bits out and trained a big neural network relying primarily on attention and a feedforward component. That beat the baseline by a lot, and now we see a further convergence to a specific architecture within the neural network space. The modern (transfer) training pipeline of LLMs was probably created by Jeremy Howard in the ULMFiT paper, which was cited as a key influence by Alec Radford who built GPT-1.   

All this was on the technical side. In terms of societal recognition, there were two major events. The first was when IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov at chess. That had hardcoded chess rules (even inside hardware-level instructions). The second was Deep Mind's AlphaGo beating the world Go champion which sent shockwaves around the world, particularly in Asia where Go is held in great esteem. Deep Mind was the first startup to explicitly pursue Artificial General Intelligence, a term coined by Shane Legg (and Ben Goertzel) - Demis allegedly met Shane at the fringe Singularity Summit (started post the popular book by Ray Kurzweil).  

That brings us to now. Multiple labs in the US and China are at the frontier which itself is changing from month to month. People are talking about the AI bubble, not knowing that all technological disruptions were preceded by a bubble and that it is the natural order of things. Countries want to restrict AI diffusion assuming it to be critical for national security, similar to how the US banned the export of cryptographic ciphers in the 90s. No one really knows how this will play out - the skill of predicting the future is drastically different from the skill of building AI models.  

What's certain though is that the story of AI is just getting started. There are too many open questions. Ever better models will keep getting trained and the impact that AI has had on the world till now, is minuscule compared to what's to follow over the next decade.  

This new AI lab will pursue AI research and play a big role in shaping AI's future for humanity. The world needs more AI labs instead of handful, and history shows us that decentralization and democratization of technologies are the best bet against authoritarian concentration.  

The best companies are started as adventures. The best reason to leave a safe job and join a risky venture, is that it will give you a unique opportunity to realize your full potential, so that you can build something of lasting value that you can point to after decades. The pursuit of excellence requires no justification and all other forms of compensation usually follow - the score always takes care of itself.
