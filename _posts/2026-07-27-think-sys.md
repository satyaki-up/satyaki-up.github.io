I've always been interested in how to think clearly and how to make the best decisions, mainly because knowledge work is all about ideas and that means that someone who's good at dealing with ideas will be better at knowledge work. There're a lot of resources online for this: [The Psychology of Human Misjudgment](https://jamesclear.com/great-speeches/psychology-of-human-misjudgment-by-charlie-munger), [Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/), [How To Decide](https://www.amazon.co.uk/How-Decide-Simple-Making-Choices/dp/0593084608), [Principles](https://www.principles.com/), [The Art of Thinking Clearly](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Art-Thinking-Clearly-Better-Decisions/dp/1444759566). What all of them have in common is that break down decision-making to atomic bits and have a _process_ for deciding.

Here's an assorted list of processes that I've found useful:  
1) Using the concept of [believability](https://www.principles.com/principles/633d5d13-8610-425f-ad62-cd62347d9165/) from Ray Dalio. For every opinion from person P about topic T, assign some score to whether P actually knows about T. Since most people have opinions about everything, it's a filter to ignore irrelevant opinions.  
2) Understanding your [role](https://www.principles.com/principles/9220c879-16c9-4734-a107-6fb0835ff25c/) in the conversation. Again, this is always in reference to a tuple (person, topic).  
3) Take criticism in the third person. If you're getting negative feedback, assume you're outside your body looking in, as an observer to this whole exchange and asking yourself, "Is the person in front of me actually doing this wrong?"  
4) Consciously rejecting feedback, or choosing not to act on it even if you agree with it. There're pros and cons to everything and if the negative behaviour is coupled with something you'd rather not lose, you're making a conscious decision not to change.  
5) Thinking of feedback as a gift, as explicitly something that'll take you to the next level. Not doing this is proven bad. [Jordan](https://x.com/RayDalio/status/1800215938261807487), [Graham](https://paulgraham.com/persistence.html)  
6) Understand both the deadline and the cost of delay of making a decision. The deadline is the time by which the decision has to be made, otherwise useless. Now, it can be made at any point before the deadline but even then, doing it quicker might be better or irrelevant.  
7) If unable to pick between two choices, it means that both are roughly equivalent and you can pick randomly.  
8) Not deciding or inertia, is itself a decision.  
9) [Inversion](https://fs.blog/inversion/). For every decision, consciously think about what the opposite entails.
